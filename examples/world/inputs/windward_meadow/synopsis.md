# Where the Meadow Listens

Windward Meadow is a broad, windswept basin of warm golden grass, orange field flowers, blue distant hills and towering cream-white clouds. A single old tree marks the western footpath. A low weather observatory stands on a rise above the meadow; its rust-red roof and teal shutters are visible from the fields. The place is original, peaceful and inhabited, with ordinary weather rather than supernatural forces.

Mira Vale, an adult woman aged twenty-seven, is the observatory's field caretaker. She has a round face, a short warm-copper bob neatly above the shoulders and an easy, attentive manner. Her fitted deep blue-teal utility jacket, cream shirt, tucked rust-orange collar accent, slate trousers and brown work boots are practical field clothes. She inspects the grass around each instrument, keeps walking routes open and repairs small failures before they distort an observation. She carries no signature prop and is recognizable through her silhouette and clothes.

The observatory is a compact stone-and-timber room under the rise's rust-red roof. Its wide open shutters admit the wind, and a sheltered threshold lets visitors watch the measuring mechanism without crossing the instrument lawn. In the room, a large plain circular counterweight and moving rods translate the outdoor instrument's motion into an audible click. Nothing about the mechanism needs writing, a screen or a diagram to be understood.

The Wind Crown is one specific weather instrument on the rise: a squat, weathered rust-red hoop with three broad teal cups rotating around an exposed axle. Its rotation pulls the observatory's rods. A short grass verge surrounds its base so Mira can hear and see friction before a slow cup becomes an incorrect calm reading. Repairing the bent axle requires the Crown to stop for a morning, leaving the meadow without its usual observations during that interval.

The Meadow Cooperative is the group of local growers and caretakers who maintain the common paths and share the meadow's harvest. A paired teal-and-rust stripe on their plain work aprons identifies cooperative work days. They need safe paths and a dry harvest, while Mira needs untrampled grass around the Crown and uninterrupted observations. They disagree constructively over the width of the protected verge, whether a crowded visitor morning should close the threshold, and whether to replace the worn axle quickly or preserve and straighten the original part. Each choice costs labor, access or missing weather observations.

The Stilling Morning was yesterday's brief interruption: the Wind Crown stopped even while the grass leaned and the great clouds continued moving. Mira found grass seed wound around the axle and cleared it with the Cooperative's help. The Crown turned again, but the exposed bend remained. The incident changed the Cooperative's priorities from simply cutting grass for access to learning where a quiet machine and a quiet day differ. Whether the old axle can be restored before the next windy harvest morning is unresolved.
